Snowball vs avalanche: two ways to order debt payoff

Published November 22, 2026

Snowball and avalanche are two common ways to decide which debt gets extra money first, after you have met the minimum payments you are required to make. They are ordering methods, not a complete plan, and they are not the only options. This article explains what each one means and the general tradeoffs, as information only. It is not financial advice, and it is not a recommendation that you use either method. For decisions about your own debts, consider speaking with a qualified professional who can look at your situation.

What both methods share

Most descriptions of snowball and avalanche start from the same place. You continue to make at least the minimum payment on each debt. Any extra amount you choose to put toward debt, above those minimums, is directed to one debt at a time, in a chosen order.

When that first debt is paid off, the extra amount, along with what you had been paying as its minimum, is often directed to the next debt in the same order,. Neither method tells you how much extra you can pay, whether you should pay extra, or whether to refinance, consolidate, or pause a payment.

Snowball: smallest balance first

In the snowball method, you list debts from the smallest remaining balance to the largest. The extra payment goes to the smallest balance, while you keep paying at least the minimum on the others.

The plain logic is about the length of the list. A smaller balance can reach zero sooner than a larger one, if the extra payment is the same. Each time a debt is paid off, you have one fewer payment to keep track of.

The tradeoff is straightforward. While extra money is going to a small balance, a debt with a higher interest rate may still be accruing interest on a larger amount. Snowball does not aim to pick the highest rate first. It aims to clear the smallest balances first. Interest rates still matter, because they are part of each debt. The snowball order simply does not use the rate as the ranking rule.

Avalanche: highest interest rate first

In the avalanche method, you list debts from the highest interest rate to the lowest. The extra payment goes to the debt with the highest rate, while you keep paying at least the minimum on the others.

The plain logic is about how interest is charged. If two balances are otherwise similar, the one with the higher rate generally costs more in interest over the same period. Directing extra payments toward the higher rate first is a way to reduce that cost, if rates and balances stay as they are and payments continue.

The tradeoff is about when the first debt comes off the list. If the highest-rate debt has a large balance, it may take longer to pay off than a small, lower-rate debt would have. The ranking rule is the rate, not the size.

How to think about the tradeoff

The usual comparison is simple, and it does not require a study or a guaranteed outcome. Snowball prioritizes the number of debts remaining, so the first debts to be paid off tend to be the smaller ones. Avalanche prioritizes the rate at which interest is charged, so extra payments go first to the debt that, by its rate, is the most expensive to carry.

Which one feels more workable can depend on how many debts you have, how different the balances and rates are, and whether you care more about crossing items off a list or about lowering interest cost over time.

If you sort the same list both ways, you can see the two orders side by side. Seeing the order is not the same as knowing what will happen to your interest or your payoff date. Those results depend on your actual rates, balances, fees, and the payments you make.

What this is not

This is general information about two common ordering methods. It is not financial advice, credit advice, or a plan for your household. It does not tell you which method to use, how much to pay, or whether to pay a given debt first. A qualified professional, such as a certified financial planner, a credit counselor, or another adviser who is qualified to look at your accounts, can help you consider your own numbers and options.
